Both sample-set formats, opened from the packages they ship in

Download a set, point Masterpiece at the file, and play. The console comes up as its producer drew it, with every coupler wired the way the definition wires it.

  • Hauptwerk-format definitions

    Full, compact and composite XML, with every console page, jamb and console size the set draws.

  • GrandOrgue organs

    .organ definitions converted as they load, console included, and every manual playable.

  • Archives opened as they are

    RAR 4 and 5, solid, multi-part and multi-volume, and GrandOrgue .orgue packages. An unpacked folder works too. WAV and WavPack samples at any rate.

  • Each set's own switch network

    Couplers, sub- and super-octaves, unison off, reversibles, crescendo and alternate ranks.

Faster than real time, virtuoso test

  1. 512 voices on an Intel i7, against the same engine without vector code; 1.7–3.3× with SSE2, about 3× with NEON on a Raspberry Pi 4.
  2. Friesach, 24-bit samples: 16.2 GB held in memory, 7.0 GB with release tails streamed.
  3. Lemmer on an eight-core i7-9700, 16-bit samples, streamed releases: each audio block computed in 5.4% of its own length on average, and the slowest 1% still 10× faster than real time. The virtuoso test: 1,518 notes in 33 seconds, up to 1,300 notes a second.
  4. Release tail cutoff: tails end once they fall below −90 dB, inaudible in every test, which frees their voices for the notes being played. At −80 dB, the virtuoso test on a two-core i3 runs with 95% fewer late blocks, and a few release recordings with a late sound in them can end a little early.

Composite organs built from several sample sets

Organs assembled from the pipework of several sets, such as Augustine's Jehmlich and Zamárdi-Buda composites, each set read from its own archive.

  • Sets found where you keep them

    The composite's folder, your package folder and your sample libraries.

  • One question per set kept elsewhere

    An archive or folder chosen once, and remembered for every later load.

  • Standard component noises

    Key action, stop action and blower noises made by Masterpiece.

The whole organ from one keyboard

Up to three split points, each zone on a manual or the pedal, with a layered second manual, its own octave and transposition, and the pedal from the lowest key held.

The full feature list

Your keyboards and pedals

A channel per manual, with key range, transposition, velocity window, short octaves and debounce. Every control can be learned from MIDI, and drawstop lamps and jamb displays answer back.

Quality presets

Best, High, Recommended, Compact, Small and Smallest, each shown with the memory this organ will take. Every setting can also be set by hand, per organ.

Stops, ranks and perspectives

Any stop, rank or microphone perspective left out of a load. A note left out borrows its neighbour's samples, retuned.

The whole organ on one panel

Stops grouped by division for sets drawn across many jambs, sized by hand or fitted to the window, and any page in a window of its own.

Temperaments, pitch and transposer

The organ's own tuning, nine historical temperaments, Scala files, any pitch for A and ±12 semitones.

A combination action on every organ you load

Generals, divisionals and a sequencer of your own sit beside the organ's pistons, including on sets that ship with few or none.

  • Up to 100 generals

    Divisionals per manual, general cancel and named combination sets, saved with each organ.

  • A 999-frame sequencer

    Next and previous on any MIDI button, with digits and Insert as on a setter board.

  • Combinaison coupler

    A Great divisional also recalls the Pedal divisional of the same number.

  • Set turns off after storing

    On every piston, from the screen, the console or MIDI.

  • Couplers where you leave them

    Pistons can leave the bass and melody couplers alone. The program couplers at 16′, 8′ and 4′ store with the stops.

  • Held notes kept through a change

    As an option, a stop change leaves the notes already held alone, and a sustain pedal holds the notes let go.

Voicing and output, rank by rank

Each control here takes no processor time until you set it, so an organ played as its producer voiced it renders exactly as before.

  • An EQ per rank and per division

    Ten presets and your own, each on a MIDI button.

  • Pan and stereo width

    Per rank and per division.

  • Attack emphasis

    Each pipe begins louder or softer and settles to its own level.

  • Pipe detune and wind sway

    Per organ, off by default.

  • Live levels

    Faders for ranks, divisions and output pairs, from MIDI knobs, the Outputs tab or the control API.

  • The output stage

    Compressor, peak limiter and output presets, bass management to a subwoofer channel, and output pairs on device channels of their own.

Scores and recordings at the console

Open a MusicXML score and the organ plays it, with a cursor on the beat. Record yourself, and the take plays back on the keyboard you played it on.

  • Score panel

    MusicXML engraved in the program, with the cursor kept in sight through every page.

  • Page-turn piston

    A console piston turns the page in a score app over MIDI.

  • MIDI recorder

    Every note, stop and shoe movement, with the console in the file, replayed under another registration or saved for a sequencer.

  • Record and Play on the console

    Both buttons learnable on any MIDI control.

  • Audio takes and a metronome

    In the organ's own audio stream.

The console on a phone, and an API for your own hardware

Turn on Remote control, and a phone or tablet on your network shows the organ's own console pages in its browser.

  • Web console

    Every console page or a stop panel per division, with volume, transposer, temperament and EQ presets, and each load with its progress.

  • One protocol over HTTP, MIDI SysEx and OSC

    Every stop, piston, level and sequencer frame, described in an OpenAPI file.

  • This computer only

    The API can listen on the computer alone.

  • Messages to the console

    Lamps lit after a load, a ready lamp, and learnable Quit and Power-off buttons for a console with no screen in reach.

Common questions

Is Masterpiece free?

Yes. Masterpiece is free and open source under the GPL-3.0 licence, with every feature included and nothing to register.

Which sample sets does it play?

Hauptwerk-format sets, from full, compact and composite definitions, and GrandOrgue organs, from .organ definitions and .orgue packages. Samples are WAV or WavPack at any rate; sets with encrypted samples stay with their publisher's own player.

Do the sets need unpacking first?

Masterpiece opens a set from its unpacked folder or straight from its package, solid, multi-part and multi-volume RAR sets included.

Does it run on a Raspberry Pi?

Yes, on Raspberry Pi OS in 64-bit and 32-bit, from a .deb package, with the NEON vector engine.

How much memory does a large organ need?

That depends on the loading settings. Friesach, 17 GB on disk, takes 16.2 GB at 24 bits, 5.4 GB at 24 bits held compressed, and 1.66 GB at 16 bits in mono held compressed. Stops and perspectives can also be left out.

Does holding samples compressed change the sound?

The sound stays identical. Each sample is held in a lossless code and decodes to the same bits as its file, at the cost of a little more processor work per voice.

Can it play my console?

Yes: a MIDI channel per manual, any keyboard or pedalboard, and MIDI learn for every stop, piston and swell shoe, consoles that send SysEx included.

Can I play with a single keyboard?

Split mode divides one keyboard into up to three zones, each on a manual or the pedal, with a layered second manual and the pedal from the lowest key held.

Is there a plugin version?

VST3, LV2 and AU plugins come beside the standalone program, for playing the organ inside a digital audio workstation.

Which systems does it run on?

Windows (x64), macOS (Apple silicon and Intel), Linux (Flatpak, .deb and tar.gz), Raspberry Pi, Android 8 and later, and iPhone and iPad with iOS 15 and later, sideloaded.

Part of OpenPipes, for organists who build their own console
